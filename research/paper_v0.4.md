# What the Indus seal texts are: a reproducible structural account
### Ayush Kumar (India). Computational assistance: Claude (Anthropic). Draft v0.4, 15 October 2026. Code and data: github.com/shaunmortal/indus — archived: doi:10.5281/zenodo.23399794 (v0.4.0; v0.3.1 = 10.5281/zenodo.23396758 contains the retracted F23 figure)

## Abstract
Using the two open machine-readable corpora of the Indus script (Mahadevan 1977, 2,409 distinct texts; ICIT/Wells, 1,918 distinct texts) and the object metadata that accompanies them (site, excavation area, period, object class, seal dimensions, animal emblem), we test, with permutation nulls and multiple-comparison correction, a set of claims that have been asserted by eye for a century. We find: (1) the short and tall stroke signs do not behave as a number system; (2) graphic resemblance between signs carries no information about their use, and an objective allograph test defended by out-of-sample prediction yields corrected sign lists (404 Mahadevan signs, 586 Wells signs); (3) the content of a seal text is independent of its emblem, find-spot and date, and dependent on the seal's shape and, suggestively, its size; (4) 96% of square-seal texts are unique identifiers, the alternation of their final signs on shared stems is at chance level (neither a Linear B-style paradigm nor stem-welded endings), and only three function classes (opener, core, closer) are reproducible; (5) no dependency beyond adjacent signs is learnable from texts of this length. The seal text is a personal identifier assembled from an opener formula, an individual element and a title block, in a system that resists syllabic decipherment because it carries no visible inflection. A sealed hold-out and a hashed registry of predictions are published for the next inscriptions to appear.

## 1. Data
- Mahadevan 1977 / IDF-80 (indusscript.in export): 3,916 lines, 2,906 objects, 14,153 tokens; site, object type, side, reading direction, field symbol, level. 2,409 distinct texts after collapsing identical texts.
- ICIT/Wells (yajnadevam open database): 2,543 objects, 592 signs; 2,375 with CISI numbers, 2,305 with measured width, height and thickness, 1,622 with iconography. Merged corpus of 5,369 objects with excavation area and period (CISI metadata). 1,918 distinct texts.
- Sign drawings: 418 Mahadevan drawings (indusscript.in); 642 Wells glyphs rendered from the ICIT font.
- Excavation catalogues: Marshall 1931, Mackay 1938, Vats 1940 (OCR from archive.org) for independent seal dimensions, joined by Mahadevan's own numbering (text = 1000 + MIC serial, 2000 + FEM serial, 4000 + EH serial; Appendix V), validated by depth-below-surface against Mahadevan's level codes.
Rules applied throughout: identical texts counted once (mass-produced tablets repeat one text up to 32 times); every effect tested within strata (site, text length, object class, and animal where relevant) by permutation of labels; Bonferroni over all tests in a family; a 30% hold-out (966 texts) sealed by SHA-256 on 2026-10-15 before any claim, never read.

## 2. Stroke signs are not a number system
The reading of the stroke signs as numerals is the standard one (Mahadevan 1977; Wells 2015, ch. 5, which tabulates their right-adjacent collocations and reads the Harappa tablet strokes as quantities in a volumetric system, ch. 4). We test it on the whole corpus rather than on selected contexts.
Complete inventory from the drawings: short strokes 1-12 in one, two and three tiers (M97-M122), tall strokes 1-5 (M86-M96), semicircles (M312-M315).
- Share of distinct texts carrying a stroke sign: seals 56%, sealings 40%, miniature tablets 41%, pottery 46%, copper tablets 48%. Not concentrated on accounting objects.
- Short-stroke values on distinct texts: 1 = 153, 3 = 114, 4 = 54, 5 = 34, 6 = 30, 7 = 64, 8 = 7, 9 = 4, 10 = 1, 12 = 52. Peaks at 7 and 12; the semicircle "tens" occur 2, 2, 1 and 1 times.
- Adjacent numeral pairs have no fixed order (tall-short 17, short-tall 9, tall-tall 12, short-short 32).
- Tall and short strokes are followed by disjoint sets of signs (M211 42 vs 2, M328 20 vs 0, M60 13 vs 0; M287 0 vs 25, M162 6 vs 38, M169 5 vs 36).
- Layout is lexical: three strokes in a row (M102) is productive, three as 2-over-1 (M103) is bound to M403 in 27 of 28 texts, at both cities and on all object classes.
- Replicated on ICIT (values 1 = 140, 3 = 116, 7 = 46, 12 = 36; orders mixed; complements split).
- Two-sided tablets separate the classes physically: stroke signs sit on side 2 (79% vs 20%), the jar closer on side 1; side-2 strokes are almost all tall and followed by the cup M328 (Harappa formula tall-N + cup, N = 3 > 2 > 4 > 1).
- Stroke value does not track seal size (tall rho +0.12, p = 0.08; short -0.04). One and two tall strokes, and three and four short strokes, are interchangeable in context by out-of-sample prediction on both corpora.
- A possible exception, found when testing Wells's vessel-volume reading (2015, ch. 4): on pottery graffiti in the merged corpus the tall strokes reach 4-5 in 16% of multi-sign, non-tally texts against 7% on tablets and 4% on seals; but the excess is carried by post-1977 material, the Mahadevan 1977 pottery (19 tokens) does not show it, and on the work set alone it is marginal (10% vs 3%, p = 0.08). Short strokes show no class difference. We record it as suggestive and register it as a prediction (analysis/numerals9_volumetric.py; prereg/predictions-3.json).
Conclusion: on seals and tablets the strokes are formulaic sign classes with favoured values; the tall class belongs to an administrative formula, the short class to name-like text. Whether the tall strokes count anything on pottery is open.

## 3. Looking alike is not behaving alike: an allograph test and corrected sign lists
For 104 Mahadevan signs (5,356 pairs): image similarity (centred 32x32 drawings, flip-invariant) and distributional similarity (left/right neighbours, position) are uncorrelated (r = 0.05, permutation null 0.00 +/- 0.05). Pairs high on both (allographs): M97/M98, the fish variants M59/M67/M70/M72, the handled jars M343/M344/M345, M387/M389, M347/M348, M326/M327, M12/M15; all mutual nearest neighbours in distribution space. Pairs high on image and low on distribution (look-alikes that are different signs): plain jar M342 vs handled jars (image 0.91-0.97, distribution 0.34-0.38); five tall vs five short strokes (1.00 vs 0.51); M149/M150 (0.94 vs 0.17).
Validation by prediction: merging the allograph pairs lowers held-out bigram perplexity from 29.0 to 26.3; merging look-alike-but-distinct pairs gives 28.5; random merges 28.6 +/- 0.3. An adoption rule (a merge must beat a random-merge null by two standard deviations on five splits) yields Mahadevan v3 with 401 signs and, on the Wells list (r = -0.01; same families recovered; perplexity 37.7 to 32.0 vs 36.9 random), Wells v3 with 586 signs. The cross-corpus bridge was audited the same way; Wells's two commonest openers (W817, W861) merge into Mahadevan's one (M267).

## 4. Physical correlates
Nulls: no sign is tied to an animal emblem (21 tests), to an excavation area at Mohenjo-daro (225 tests over 8 areas) or to a period (Intermediate vs Late; Harappa 3B vs 3C; 76 tests each); no sign differs between the two cities on seals (55 tests). One Harappa survivor (M176 on Mound F, ratio 1.23) persists among HARP-dated objects only.
Positives: round seals (no emblem, 201 of 1,250) use a partly different lexicon at every site (M169, M387, W154 round-favoured, 3-4x; M343, M161, M402 square-only; M169 is the round-seal closer). Among 876 steatite square seals with measured faces, within site x length x animal strata, the handled jar M343 sits on seals about 25% smaller (-172 mm2, p = 0.0003, Bonferroni-corrected over 36 signs); the plain jar closer has no size effect; the effect survives restriction to unique-text seals (p < 0.0001). Independent 1930s measurements (492 seals) show the same direction at smaller magnitude (-13%, one-sided p = 0.10), so the size result is suggestive. Seals carrying a repeated (title-like) text are 14% smaller than seals with a unique text (p = 0.003).

## 5. What the texts are
- Uniqueness: 1,296 square seals carry 1,219 distinct texts; 96% occur on one seal; round seals 95%; tags 71%; moulded tablets 44%; incised tablets 36% (one text on up to 32 tablets).
- Endings: stems attested with two different final signs number 64 against 58.6 +/- 2.4 when final signs are shuffled within length (ratio 1.09); at the start 119 vs 116; within the closer set 20 vs 15; in the pre-closer slot 22 vs 21.5. Alternation is at chance level or slightly above it: there is no small closed paradigm of endings alternating on shared stems (the Kober configuration), and no evidence that endings are welded to stems either. (An earlier draft and three emails reported '11 sd fewer than chance'; that figure came from duplicate texts surviving in the null and is retracted.)
- Three classes: stability-selected clustering of 73 frequent signs (20 resamples per K) supports openers (9 signs), closers (13) and a core (51); adjusted Rand 0.59 at K = 3 against 0.31 at K = 12.
- Depth: held-out perplexity unigram 87, bigram 48, trigram 51, class models 85-96; multi-sign units add nothing (bits per sign 4.711 to 4.700); recurring blocks cover 59% of tokens against 63% for bigram-sampled corpora.
- Composition: 45% of 2-3-sign texts occur verbatim inside longer texts (null 29%), mostly at the end; among unique names the 3-sign ending before the closer is shared more than a bigram process predicts (z +4.3) and the beginning less (z -11); the shared endings are title blocks with their own fixed closer (fish-M336-M89 + M211 on 21 names; fish-M249-M161 + jar on 12, one at Kish; M51-M130-M149 + jar on 10, one at Rakhigarhi).
- Identical texts recur across cities above chance (77 vs 49 +/- 4.5), but the cases are moulded tablets and short title strings; the 5-6-sign texts attested twice are the personal-name candidates.
Picture: seal text = opener formula + individual element + title block + closer; identity is combinatorial; the system carries no visible inflection and no dependency beyond adjacency.

## 6. Consequences for decipherment
A syllabic decipherment of the Kober-Ventris kind needs a small closed set of endings alternating on shared stems far above chance; the corpus shows alternation at chance level, spread over many signs. Statistical key-fitting cannot recover even a planted language on texts of this length (Garg 2026, reproduced in spirit by our depth results). What remains is the anchor route: one object joining an Indus text to a readable name or quantity. We have exhausted the published Ur material (UE III, IV, VII, X) and the Gulf, Iranian and Central Asian catalogues; none exists in print. The structural account above is what makes an anchor usable: a corrected sign list, three defensible slots, a title-block inventory (data/title_blocks_v1.json), and a frozen set of eight predictions (prereg/predictions-1.json, SHA-256 published) for the next inscriptions from Failaka, Rakhigarhi or elsewhere.

## 6b. Relation to the 'branding' reading of the seal system
Frenez (2026, Archaeologies) reads the Indus stamp seal as an institutional branding instrument: extreme formal standardisation, one dominant motif sustained as an unbalanced oligopoly, seals as procedural tokens of authorised roles rather than emblems of individual identity. Our results split the object into two channels that behave differently. The motif channel is as Frenez describes it: standardised, dominated by the unicorn, and, in our tests, statistically independent of the text (no sign-emblem association, F14). The text channel is the opposite: 96% of square-seal texts occur on one seal only, so whatever a seal brands, its inscription individuates. The two readings are compatible if the motif marks the authorising institution and the text the authorised person or office; two of our measurements fit that division: seals whose text repeats (title-like strings) are 14% smaller than seals with a unique text, and the handled-jar sign that marks smaller seals is a mid-text element rather than the closer. Round seals, which drop the motif and shift the lexicon, would then be the case where the institutional channel is absent and only the personal one remains. These are structural inferences, not readings; they are testable on the next excavated lot through the registry in section 6.

## Appendix A. The Tamil Nadu graffiti 'parallels'
The Tamil Nadu State Department of Archaeology database (tngraffiti.in; 42 base signs, 5,163 occurrences across 140 sites) is cited for a 60-90% morphological parallel with Indus signs. With the same image features as section 3, 30 of the 42 base signs have an Indus look-alike at or above the top-5% similarity level among Indus pairs, and 9 at the top-1% level; the strong matches are single strokes, triangles and arrow shapes. The behavioural test is possible because 795 graffiti carry two base signs: mapping each Tamil sign to its best Indus look-alike, the Tamil sign pairs are adjacent in Indus texts no more often than frequency-matched random mappings (23.9% vs 20.1% +/- 6.3%; with the top three look-alikes 55.0% vs 56.2% +/- 4.4%; p = 0.51). The commonest Tamil pairs map to Indus pairs that are never adjacent. The morphological parallel is real as a statement about shapes and carries no detectable continuity of use, which is what section 3 shows for shapes inside the Indus corpus itself.

## 7. Limits
The ICIT/Wells corpus used here is a snapshot redistributed some years ago without the compilers' permission (A. Fuls, pers. comm., 15 Oct 2026); it is partly outdated relative to the final ICIT (3,903 artifacts, 4,794 texts, 695 signs: Wells 2015, preface) and flags every text as complete, so it cannot separate complete texts from possibly complete fragments. All ICIT figures in this paper are therefore replications, and the primary results rest on Mahadevan 1977, whose concordance marks doubtful signs; dropping the 11% of lines with a doubtful sign leaves the ending-alternation ratio (1.09), the nesting share (39% vs 38%) and the stroke-value profile unchanged (analysis/robust_doubtful.py). Drawings, not photographs, for the image tests; ICIT dimensions of unknown provenance (possibly from the same 1930s catalogues); OCR recall on those catalogues 20-70%; small samples on round seals abroad; the hold-out remains unread, so nothing here is a prediction test yet.
